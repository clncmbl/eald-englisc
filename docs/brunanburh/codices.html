Medieval Codices

Terminology

"Book" vs "Manuscript" vs "Codex"

A physical medieval book might be referred to as a "book", a "manuscript", or as a "codex". For example, the four most important physical books of Old English poetry are known as the Exeter Book, the Junius Manuscript, the Vercelli Book, and the Nowell Codex. (Incidentally, these are not quoted or italized because they are not titles but are, rather, proper names for those physical objects.) The word "book" can refer to a physical object, but it can also refer to content that could be published in the form of physical books. The word "manuscript" can refer to any handwritten document. It could take the form of a scroll, a physical book, or a set of loose papers, for example. The word "codex", on the other hand, is more specific. It refers to a bound stack of writing material (paper or parchment, for example) bound together at one edge. Most commonly, it refers to an ancient or medieval physical book containing manuscript. Consequently, in the absence of a fixed, consistent naming convention for a specific physical object, I prefer to refer to a physical medieval book as a "codex" (with plural "codices").

Medieval Codex Pagination

The pages of medieval codices are typically numbered by "folio" rather than page number. The word "folio" comes from the Latin word for leaf. A folio is a single leaf. Scribes could fold a sheet of writing surface (parchment, for example) into two or more folios depending on how many times it is folded. A folio that is not part of a folded sheet is called a "singleton" and indicates that something unexpected happened during the production process requiring the addition or removal of a folio. A folio can have two pages—the front and the back of the leaf. A specific page is referenced using the folio number followed by an "r" or a "v". The "r" is an abbreviation for "recto", which is Latin for "right", meaning "correct" or "upright". The "v" is an abbreviation for "verso", which comes from Latin "to turn". So, "r" is the primary or upright side of a leaf, and "v" is what you get if you turn over that leaf. Therefore, "folio 26r" refers to the first side of the 26th leaf and "folio 26v" refers to the second side of that same leaf.
